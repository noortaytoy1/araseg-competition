\begin{abstract}
The Araseg Shared task aims at developing arabic sentence segmentation models. Literature argues that training tricks for deep learning models can further develop and push for further F1. However, the problem with such models is that we do not know why such a model makes confident mistakes. Because of this issue, we present the ENSAR system submitted to the AraSeg 2026 shared task on arabic sentence segmenation, where we argue that the next step for better performance models are reasoning models. Given an input sentence, the goal of a sentence segmentation model is to classify a subword whether it is a boundary (difference between contexts) or not by training a binary classifier. Our proposed system outperforms all models on 3 out of 4 tracks on the blind test set.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
This paper presents our participation in the AraSeg 2026 Shared Task on Arabic
Sentence Segmentation \citep{elkholy-etal-2026-araseg-shared-task}, organized as part
of ArabicNLP 2026 where the task is to label every whitespace token of a document as sentence-final or
not, across four tracks that cross punctuation with paragraph structure. \citet{read2012sentence} showed that
treating segmentation as terminal-mark disambiguation yields overly optimistic
estimates and degrades, drastically for some systems, away from edited newswire. In
Arabic, punctuation is used inconsistently and with ambiguous force, many genres carry
no dependable boundary marker at all
\citep{elkholy2026arabicsentencesegmentationgenres}, the conjunction \textit{wa} opens
sentences as readily as it joins clauses, and in the no-punctuation tracks a boundary
has no surface mark whatsoever. Hence, unlike other languages, arabic punctuation has proven to be much harder than its English counterparts.

Our system pairs an encoder ensemble with a reasoning stage that learns each genre's
annotation convention explicitly where we develop five fine-tuned encoders that propose boundaries through
a length-aware semi-Markov decode, and two LLM juries, each holding a policy file
written from its own graded errors on the training split, debate and argue each document and
apply only unanimous edits. The design follows from a measurement in
(Appendix~\ref{sec:erroranalysis}) where 48.3\% of the ensemble's development errors,
1{,}811 of 3{,}749, are errors where all five voters agree with each other and disagree
with the annotator confidently; no threshold, calibration, or added voter reaches these. Hence, we propose adding a second stage trained LLM juries that reason about the sentence and genre structure of the document, and through reasoning traces, it decides which ensemble predictions are fine to keep and which ones it missed.

Our system placed first on three of the four tracks in both the closed and open
settings; on the fourth, NoPnx-PA, it did not place first, its juries rebuilt in the
final hours before the deadline on only 60 of the 174 training documents. After training it to its full potential, the results were promising. We have released our code, the full prompts, and all six learned policies
at \url{https://github.com/noortaytoy1/araseg-competition}.

Our main contributions are: (i) a training loop whose improved object is a written
set of instructions, with the annotator's answer at every token as feedback on the juries's answers
(Section~\ref{sec:loop}); (ii) an adjudication step in which two policies trained on
disjoint corpus halves argue each document, applying only edits that both endorse and agree on
(Section~\ref{sec:adj}); (iii) a controlled measurement of the reasoning layer
against an untrained reasoning model showing the importance of understanding arabic structure
(Section~\ref{sec:results}); and (iv) the learned policy files themselves, stating
where a sentence ends in each kind of text together with the errors that taught it
(Section~\ref{sec:inside}).
\section{Background}
\paragraph{Arabic sentence segmentation.} The literature offers two answers to
missing punctuation: restore it, so boundaries follow from the predicted terminal
marks \citep{tilk2016bidirectional}, or classify boundaries directly. WtP trains a
punctuation-agnostic character classifier on newline self-supervision across 85
languages \citep{minixhofer2023wheres}, SaT hardens it with a pretraining scheme
built for missing punctuation and outperforms all baselines, including strong LLMs, on
poorly formatted text \citep{frohmann2024segment}, and ersatz classifies candidate
punctuation contexts across a 23-language evaluation set \citep{wicks2021unified}. However, we argue that all of these methods are classifiers where we cannot understand why they made a wrong boundary classification,
nor which convention the document follows; the corpus paper reports cross-genre
generalization as still challenging.
\paragraph{Learning in text with frozen weights.} Our Juries (the LLMs) training structure immitates RLHF's reward and
policy improvement while discarding the gradient
\citep{christiano2017deep, ouyang2022training}. Because the juries are closed source models, the parameters never change and the
policy lives verbally in text. Reflexion \citep{shinn2023reflexion} reinforces an agent
verbally from task outcomes, and Self-Refine \citep{madaan2023selfrefine} iterates a
model against its own critique; The annotator's label serve as the answer key for the juries to learn from their mistakes. ExpeL \citep{zhao2024expel} distils textual insights from agent trajectories (reasoning) and Agent Workflow Memory \citep{wang2024awm} induces reusable workflows; we apply the same idea to Arabic sentence segmentation, with token-level gold as the grading signal, to test whether it yields further improvements over a strong encoder ensemble since the next step for arabic sentence segmentation models are models that show why they classified a specific boundary. OPRO
\citep{yang2024optimizers} and TextGrad \citep{yuksekgonul2024textgrad} optimise
prompts from aggregate signals, not a persistent domain statement. Beyond the AraSeg
corpus, our only resources are four pretrained encoders, AraBERTv0.2 base and large
\citep{antoun-etal-2020-arabert}, Arabic-BERT-large \citep{safaya-etal-2020-kuisail},
and XLM-RoBERTa-large \citep{conneau-etal-2020-unsupervised}, and one pretrained LLM,
Claude Opus 5; no lexicons, dialect data, or external corpora.
\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
The AraSeg corpus consists of manually annotated Modern Standard Arabic documents from
eight genres, including scripture, classical narrative, children's readers, and exam
sheets \citep{elkholy2026arabicsentencesegmentationgenres}. Each track provides 174
training, 222 development, and 262 test documents, plus blind sets of 212
(no-paragraph) and 100 (paragraph-aware) documents; boundaries are sparse,
8.3--10.4\% of tokens by track. The four tracks cross punctuation (Pnx/NoPnx) with
paragraph structure (PA/NP). In the paragraph-aware tracks, paragraph breaks survive
as newline tokens where the token before a break always ends a sentence and a break token never carries a boundary; the final token of every document ends a sentence. The corpus contains no dialectal text, Arabizi, or code-switching, and we treat all
documents identically.
\section{System Overview}
\subsection{Encoder ensemble}
We fine-tune the four encoders into five voters: AraBERTv0.2-large twice, with FGM
adversarial training at $\epsilon = 1.0$ \citep{miyato2017adversarial} and with an
auxiliary supervised contrastive loss at weight 0.1 \citep{khosla2020supervised}, the
remaining three at defaults. Every voter trains 10
epochs on overlapping 180-word windows, stride 90, with cross-entropy weighting the
boundary class by the negative-to-positive ratio capped at 8 (8.00 on every track); probabilities average across voters and windows; both
regularizers act on the training split only, and the ensemble uses no resource beyond
the training data. Rather than thresholding, we decode with a
semi-Markov dynamic program \citep{sarawagi2004semimarkov},
$\hat{B} = \arg\max_{B} \sum_{i \in B} \log p_i + \lambda \sum_{s \in \mathrm{seg}(B)} \mathcal{L}(|s|) - \beta |B|$
with $\lambda = 0.4$ and $\beta = 0.75$, which trades boundary probability against
segment-length plausibility under a training-split prior: a boundary is kept when its
probability and the lengths of the segments it creates are jointly more believable
than leaving it out. The length prior is refitted on the first-pass output (blended with
the corpus prior at 0.7) and the document is decoded again; structurally certain
boundaries are forced. For the Pnx-PA submission we used a tuned three-voter subset
with $\beta = 0.6$. No single voter matches the ensemble (solo
macro $F_1$ 80.74--84.56 against 85.62 on NoPnx-NP development).
\subsection{Learning a policy from graded errors}
\label{sec:loop}
A jury is defined entirely by one text file, which it writes itself; training
proceeds in batches of five documents. \textbf{Attempt:} the jury reads its
current policy and segments each document from scratch, recording each boundary as a
token index plus the token. \textbf{Reward:} the attempt is scored against the
training gold, and then
\textbf{Diagnosis:} every false cut and miss is returned in context, the jury's
answer beside the annotator's at the same place in the text. \textbf{Policy update:}
the jury writes the rule behind each error into its file, its own failure as the
worked example; contradicted entries are revised in place. \textbf{Reset:} the
documents are discarded, and the next batch begins from the updated policy with no
other memory. Nothing else survives between batches: whatever the jury learns must be written
down, forcing the policy to be explicit and inspectable. We train two juries on disjoint halves of the corpus, 87 documents each,
so their policies genuinely differ; the NoPnx-PA files run 1{,}398 and 2{,}098 lines.
Superseded rules are struck through, not deleted, and every revision names the errors
that forced it (one update and two full arcs verbatim in
Appendix~\ref{sec:learning}, Figure~\ref{fig:update}).
\subsection{Adjudication: only edits both juries endorse}
\label{sec:adj}
At inference the juries take one document at a time, the ensemble's boundaries
pre-marked and described as an unreliable cheat sheet to consult but never trust;
each states its reading from its own policy and they argue every site on which they
differ, rules earned from graded errors in the register outranking unsupported
intuition. Only edits both juries endorse
apply; unresolved disagreements leave the draft, and an unchanged document is
admissible. Application is strict, an edit landing only
where the stated index holds exactly the stated token (met by 3{,}258 of 3{,}258
evaluation edits), and all reported $F_1$ comes from the organizers' script. Training and inference deliberately differ: the jury trains from scratch, since one
that only corrected drafts could learn to defer to them, and the learned statement of
where sentences end applies to writing and judging alike; Table~\ref{tab:ablation}
shows it transfers. Both jury stages run Claude Opus 5 (model string \texttt{claude-opus-5}) at
maximum reasoning effort with provider-default sampling (no temperature or top-p
set); full prompts are provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}.
\section{Experimental Setup}
All final models are trained on the training split only; the
development split serves for checkpoint and hyperparameter selection, never as
training data; the juries learn from training documents and gold alone; and the
released test split feeds the controlled measurements in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, as
organizer rules permit. We entered all
four tracks, closed and open; the organizers ruled post-editing an encoder system with
a pretrained LLM permitted in both settings. % FILL-6: ruling date + channel

We apply no text normalization: alef and ya variants, ta marbuta, diacritics, and
tatweel are preserved, since the token sequence is the label space. Each encoder
applies its own subword tokenizer over the organizer-provided whitespace tokens, the
word-level label riding the last subword. Training and inference
use Python 3.13.3, PyTorch 2.11.0+cu128 and Hugging Face Transformers 4.55.0, the
version recorded inside every released checkpoint; no Farasa or CAMeL
Tools preprocessing. All jury prompts are in English over the Arabic documents;
we did not try Arabic-language prompts.

Encoders were fine-tuned on Nvidia's RTX 5090 GPU, at roughly 4 minutes per voter per
track (full recipes in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}); juries ran through the provider
API at about six wall-clock minutes per document per lane, four lanes in parallel.
Every reported number is a single run; run-to-run variability is measured by a second
full adjudication run on 50 test documents (Limitations), and we audited the
file-access transcripts of every run (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}).
\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\paragraph{Official results.}
Our system achieved first place on the official leaderboards of Pnx-PA, Pnx-NP, and
NoPnx-NP, closed and open, with blind $F_1$ of 95.30, 92.0, and 89.1. On NoPnx-PA it scored 90.0 and did not place first, its
juries rebuilt in the final hours on 60 of 174 training documents.
\paragraph{Ablation setup.}
The leaderboard cannot separate the two stages, so we compare arms on the released
test split: identical documents, gold, draft, prompts, unanimity rule, and model,
differing only in jury context. In the \textbf{zero-shot} arm the doctrine step is
removed: juries are told they are untrained and edit from their own knowledge of
Arabic alone. The
\textbf{doctrine} arm is the submitted system. Part of the treatment: without two distinct doctrines the juries are nearer two
copies of one policy, so the unanimity filter is expected weaker there.

NoPnx-PA is the post-deadline retrain, and the ablation baseline is a
thresholded average, about half a point below the submitted decode on NoPnx; neither
affects the sign or significance of the differences.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}
\paragraph{A policy, not a threshold.}
The most diagnostic numbers in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} are the edit directions:
NoPnx-NP adds 923 boundaries and removes 417; NoPnx-PA adds 547 and removes 722. The ensemble under-segments when punctuation and paragraph structure are
both absent and over-segments when paragraph tokens are present, and the juries
correct each; no threshold, temperature, or decoding penalty yields opposite
corrections from one system, so the layer conditions on what the document is, a
policy rather than a confidence adjustment.
On the punctuated tracks the doctrine juries make a fraction of the edits (395 and
254 against 1{,}340 and 1{,}269), leave more than half of documents untouched (144
and 160 of 262), and the gain falls to under one point, still positive: the policy
substitutes for missing punctuation rather than duplicating it. Degradations concentrate where both juries shared a reading the
annotators did not: unanimity filters disagreement, not shared error, which bounds
what this design can fix.
\paragraph{Scaffolding versus policy.} The zero-shot arm decomposes the doctrine
gain: on NoPnx-NP, +2.96 of the +4.85 is the model-and-debate scaffolding alone, the
remaining +1.89 the learned policy, whose margin over zero-shot is positive on all
four tracks (+1.89 to +3.88).
On the punctuated tracks zero-shot is actively harmful ($-$1.42 NP, $-$2.38 PA), and
the edit counts show why: 780 and 760 edits against the doctrine's 395 and 254 on
already-strong drafts. The learned policy teaches restraint as much as action; the arm
ordering shows the restraint is learned, not innate to the model or debate format.
\paragraph{Memorization.} The NoPnx gold is the source's punctuation deleted,
and the sources are public, so the model might recall the marks rather than infer the
convention. Such recall lives in the weights, not the doctrine, and would surface in
the zero-shot arm; instead zero-shot trails the doctrine everywhere and the ensemble
on two tracks, and the degraded documents rule it out by construction: recall would
reproduce the gold exactly there.
\paragraph{What the learned policies contain.}
\label{sec:inside}
The policies are plain text, so we can read what the system learned. One jury's first
rule for any new document is to count words between paragraph breaks, cutting at the
breaks and almost nowhere else when lines are short, written after it scored 33.3 on a
multiple-choice exam bank under prose rules. It keeps genre-specific length
expectations (about 10.6 words per sentence for a children's picture book, 14.3 for a
children's magazine, 11 for classical folk narrative, 20.3 for a translated
monograph), split after treating them alike cost points.
Appendix~\ref{sec:learning} shows a rule induced, refuted, and retired in writing
(Figure~\ref{fig:update}); late in training both juries independently made ``what
kind of text is this'' their explicit first step. None of this is expressible by our encoders, and none of it was written by us.
\section{Conclusion}
\looseness=-1
We presented ENSAR, an encoder ensemble refined by a reasoning layer whose policy is
learned from graded errors and stored as text, not weights, first on three of four AraSeg
2026 tracks. A controlled ablation credits the layer with close to
five macro-$F_1$ points where punctuation is absent and under one where present,
corrections reversing by track and untrained juries hurting the punctuated tracks. Meaningful headroom in Arabic segmentation is thus definitional: genre
conventions recoverable from graded errors and statable in language; future work will
extend the loop to dialectal Arabic, other languages, and cheaper carriers for written
policies.
\section*{Limitations}
The reasoning layer is expensive: adjudicating one document costs on the order of tens
of thousands of tokens, several orders of magnitude more than an encoder forward pass,
so the method suits settings where segmentation quality outweighs throughput. The
juries are a proprietary model accessed through an API, decoding is not deterministic,
and exact reproduction depends on that model version remaining available; we release
the learned policies and the prompts so the stage can be inspected even where it
cannot be re-derived bit for bit. A second full run on 50 NoPnx-NP test documents
differed from the first by 0.48 $F_1$; the effect is an order of magnitude larger than
the run-to-run variability. When both juries share a wrong reading, nothing in the
design catches it, and every one of our degraded documents is of that kind. All
results are from one corpus in one language. Both juries are simulated within a
single model context per document, each constrained to argue from its own policy file;
their independence lies in the separately trained policies, not in separate processes.
The two punctuated tracks are adjudicated by one shared jury pair whose doctrine
carries format-specific law sections, so their two rows in Table~
ef{tab:ablation}
are correlated measurements of one policy under two format laws. We did not test
conveying the conventions as raw gold-segmented examples in context; whether the loop
outperforms that simpler channel is left open.
\section*{Ethical Considerations}
Sentence segmentation is a low-risk preprocessing task, and we see no direct misuse
surface for this system. The corpus is provided by the organizers under its license
(MIT) and contains public texts including scripture and classical literature;
our system reads these texts to place boundary labels and neither alters nor generates
their content. No personal data is collected or processed. The corpus is Modern
Standard Arabic, so dialectal varieties are not represented, and we make no claim that
the learned conventions transfer to them. The reasoning layer's API cost creates an
access asymmetry for groups without such budgets; the encoder-only ensemble is the
cheap alternative, at the measured cost in accuracy, and is released.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:appendix}
\paragraph{Voter recipes.} All five voters: 10 epochs, windows of 180 words with stride 90,
maximum sequence length 512, cross-entropy with the boundary class weighted by
$\min(\mathrm{neg}/\mathrm{pos}, 8)$, which evaluates to 8.00 on every track. Defaults are
learning rate $5{\times}10^{-5}$ and batch size 16. Differences: \texttt{imp\_fgm} is
AraBERTv0.2-large with FGM $\epsilon = 1.0$; \texttt{boost\_supcon01} is AraBERTv0.2-large
with supervised contrastive weight 0.1; \texttt{xlmr-2e5} is XLM-RoBERTa-large at learning
rate $2{\times}10^{-5}$ and batch size 4; \texttt{nat\_arabertv02\_base} is
AraBERTv0.2-base and \texttt{nat\_asafaya\_large} is Arabic-BERT-large, both at defaults.
Checkpoints are selected on the development split. For Pnx-PA a tuned three-voter subset
with $\beta = 0.6$ was used at submission.
The four base checkpoints, pinned to the exact Hugging Face revisions our fine-tunes
downloaded, are \texttt{aubmindlab/bert-base-arabertv02} (revision \texttt{016fb9d6}),
\texttt{aubmindlab/bert-large-arabertv02} (\texttt{12c9edc7}),
\texttt{asafaya/bert-large-arabic} (\texttt{a000d4b3}) and
\texttt{FacebookAI/xlm-roberta-large} (\texttt{c23d21b0}). Optimization is AdamW with
weight decay 0.01, warmup ratio 0.1 and linear decay, fp16 mixed precision, seed 42,
maximum sequence length 512, batch size 16 and learning rate $5{\times}10^{-5}$ except
where a recipe above overrides them; the epoch checkpoint with the best development
$F_1$ is kept. The training entry point and the exact five launch commands are released
(\texttt{src/train\_encoder.py}, \texttt{scripts/train\_pnx\_voters.sh}).

\paragraph{Corpus statistics.} Training boundary rate is 0.104 (NoPnx-NP), 0.101
(NoPnx-PA), 0.086 (NP) and 0.083 (PA), over 98{,}457, 101{,}784, 124{,}444 and 128{,}173
tokens respectively.
\paragraph{Preprocessing and tools.}
Documents are used exactly as distributed: no normalization of alef or ya forms, ta
marbuta, diacritics or tatweel, since the token sequence is the label space and must be
preserved. Each encoder uses its own subword tokenizer over the organizer-provided
whitespace tokens, and the word-level boundary label is carried by the last subword of
each word. Training and inference use Python 3.13.3, PyTorch 2.11.0+cu128 and Hugging Face
Transformers 4.55.0, as recorded in the released checkpoints; scoring uses the
organizers' evaluation script.
\paragraph{Hardware and runs.}
Encoders were fine-tuned on an NVIDIA RTX 5090 (32\,GB), roughly 4 minutes per voter
per track; each voter is a single run, and no encoder result is an average. Jury
training and adjudication ran through the provider API at maximum reasoning effort, at
about six minutes of wall-clock per document per lane, four lanes in parallel. All
reported jury results are single runs; run-to-run variability is bounded by the
50-document rerun reported in Limitations.
\paragraph{Metric.} We report macro $F_1$, the per-document mean, computed with the
organizers' evaluation script; it is the official metric. Token-pooled micro values
are higher because errors concentrate in long documents: on test the ensemble scores
86.23 micro against 84.64 macro on NoPnx-NP, 89.30 against 87.05 on NoPnx-PA, 93.76
against 92.83 on NP, and 95.76 against 94.56 on PA.
\paragraph{Execution environment and replication.} The juries execute as agents of the
Claude Code framework (public CLI, version 2.1.234 as recorded in the run transcripts),
model \texttt{claude-opus-5} at maximum reasoning effort. That model string is
complete: the Claude~5 family publishes no dated snapshot variants, and the provider's
response metadata across our run records names the same identifier for every call. No
sampling parameter is set anywhere in the pipeline; temperature, top-$p$ and all other
decoding controls remain at provider defaults, so jury decoding is nondeterministic.
Each sitting of four documents runs in a fresh context with no memory of any other
sitting, two to four sittings in parallel; the framework-free API runner used for
replication pins \texttt{max\_tokens} to 32{,}000 with a 30{,}000-token thinking
budget. The release makes the full
pipeline repeatable at two levels: every reported number re-derives from released
artifacts alone (exact draft rows, all 1{,}048 doctrine verdicts and all 1{,}048
zero-shot verdicts, the doctrines, and a strict scorer; the packet builder reproduces
our sealed exam packets byte for byte), and the pipeline re-runs end to end from a
Makefile (corpus fetch, voter training, probability caching, packet construction), with
the jury stage driven either by a single released orchestrator prompt pasted into
Claude Code or by a framework-free API runner. The verification script re-derives every
number in this paper from source data on each build.

\paragraph{Isolation.} Jury agents run under a released file-access guard (a
pre-tool-use hook) that denies any path outside their test packets, their own output
directory, and their assigned doctrine files (the zero-shot arm assigns none); shell
access to the corpus, the gold archive and the repository is denied, and the gold is
read in memory by the scoring loop only. Transcript audits of every evaluation run
(275 agent transcripts for the zero-shot arm alone) show no access beyond the permitted
paths and no gold label vector in any agent context. The agent platform may inject
brief session notes into agent context; audits show the only such note that ever
appeared named the existence of a training curriculum and carried no corpus content,
labels, conventions, or results. The juries' learned files are themselves auditable text: every document id cited and every Arabic 8-gram quoted in any released doctrine occurs in the training split, and none occurs only in the development or test data; every graded training attempt on disk is a training-split document (machine-checked on every build).
\paragraph{Jury prompts.} The prompts below are the complete operative texts of the
system: everything a user needs to run it is these four blocks plus the released
kits. Local file paths and per-jury document lists are replaced by placeholders in
angle brackets; the per-track instantiations (format clause, never-clause, grader
command, curriculum lists) ship with the code, and a single released orchestrator

\section{Error analysis}
\label{sec:erroranalysis}
All numbers in this section are recomputed from the cached voter probabilities and the
released gold by our verification script; the setting is the NoPnx-NP track with each
voter and the ensemble mean thresholded at 0.5. On the development split the ensemble
makes 3{,}749 errors, and 1{,}811 of them (48.3\%) are sites where all five voters are
on the wrong side of the threshold: the models agree with each other and disagree with
the annotator. At the remaining sites the voters split, but no voter is worth
promoting, since every voter alone scores below the ensemble (solo macro $F_1$ from
80.74 to 84.56 against the ensemble's 85.62), so the dissenting vote at a mixed site
is not a signal a fixed combination rule can exploit.

The confident half cannot be thresholded away either. The median ensemble probability
at a false positive site is 0.86, while 22.7\% of true boundaries fall below that
value: a global threshold high enough to remove the typical false positive forfeits
nearly a quarter of the real boundaries, and calibration rescales these probabilities
monotonically and therefore cannot reorder them. Nor are the errors spread evenly; the
worst fifth of development documents holds 51.4\% of all error sites, so failure
concentrates in particular documents, which is what a method that conditions on the
document, rather than sharpening a shared probability model, is positioned to fix.

The test split shows the juries doing exactly that. Of the ensemble's 3{,}413 error
sites, the juries corrected 1{,}110 and introduced 230 new errors. 299 of the
corrected sites (26.9\%) are sites where every voter had been wrong, a class that no
reweighting, recalibration, or voter promotion can reach, because no voter disagrees
with the mistake; the remainder are mixed sites where the juries sided with the
minority reading when the document's convention licensed it. The zero-shot arm reaches
fewer errors of every kind on the same split: it corrected 917 sites while introducing
187, and 227 of its corrections (24.8\%) were all-five-wrong sites against the
doctrine's 299 (26.9\%), so the learned policy both edits more and aims slightly
better.
\section{The juries' reasoning while learning}
\label{sec:learning}
The policy files are the training record: every entry carries the batch that produced
it, and superseded rules are struck through rather than deleted. Figure~\ref{fig:update}
reproduces one abridged entry; below it we give the central revision arc of the
NoPnx-PA Jury 0 doctrine and two entries from Jury 1, verbatim except that Arabic
examples are transliterated or elided and long passages are abridged. One reading note
is essential: NoPnx-PA input carries no punctuation at all (the training split holds
101{,}784 tokens and not one punctuation mark), so when the jury below speaks of
commas and full stops it is reasoning about the marks the source text must have
carried before they were stripped, inferred from where the annotators' boundaries
fall. That inference is the point: the gold is the source's punctuation with the marks
deleted, and the jury is reconstructing the deleted convention.

the draft standing; and a structural correction argued from syntax rather than surface
pattern.
